\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Working draft: the scenario is illustrative. Methods and evaluation remain
% under discussion; the research agenda below is not a completed contribution list.
Research agents must turn research questions into decisions about which prior
work to read, which resources to adopt, and how to use them. These decisions draw
on papers, datasets, benchmarks, and code repositories, each of which provides
part of the information needed to pursue a research idea. Supporting agents in
this process motivates us to reconsider how published research is prepared for
subsequent discovery and use. Alongside efforts to improve the delivery of
research artifacts to agents~\cite{2026-ARA}, we investigate how information
already available in papers and associated materials can be organized into
reusable research knowledge.

Consider a researcher developing a method to improve long-context instruction
following and asking an agent to investigate prior work and prepare an
experimental evaluation. The request unfolds into several questions:
\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Q1---Discover.} Which studies are relevant to this idea, and which should
I read first?\\[2pt]
\textbf{Q2---Assess.} How do their research problems and experimental settings
compare with those of my proposed study?\\[2pt]
\textbf{Q3---Select.} Which benchmarks and datasets are suitable for evaluating
my method, and how have prior studies used them?\\[2pt]
\textbf{Q4---Prepare to use.} Which implementations and evaluation tools can I
adopt, and what must I check before using them?
\end{quote}
The desired outcome is a justified selection of prior work and resources,
together with the information needed to proceed. The questions can also prompt
revisions: a mismatch in experimental conditions may redirect the literature
search, while inspecting an implementation may change the choice of benchmark.

This scenario highlights three requirements. First, relevance depends on the
researcher's intended use. A study with similar terminology is a candidate for
inspection; selecting it requires understanding its problem, assumptions, and
experimental context. Second, the evidence for a decision spans different
materials. Answering Q3 and Q4 may require combining an account of a benchmark
in a paper with dataset documentation and repository instructions. Third, some
of the resulting understanding can serve subsequent questions and tasks. A
resource's purpose, documented uses, and experimental settings may remain useful
beyond the interaction in which they were first examined.

These requirements expose a gap between access to research materials and access
to knowledge prepared for research decisions. An agent can search for and read
original materials, but must still identify relevant details, interpret their
roles, and assemble evidence for its choices. When this understanding remains
confined to an individual interaction, later tasks may repeat parts of the same
work. A persistent resource library offers an opportunity to retain and reuse
that understanding. The central question is how to construct and organize such
knowledge so that it supports an agent's evolving research needs while retaining
the evidence and conditions needed to judge its applicability.

Addressing this question presents three challenges. First, \emph{construction
must capture information that supports research decisions}. Identifying a
benchmark or repository provides an entry point, but Q3 requires understanding
what it evaluates and how it has been used. The library must distinguish a
resource's stated purpose from uses documented in subsequent studies. Whether
it suits a new research idea remains a judgment to be made using that evidence.

Second, \emph{organization and access must accommodate different research
needs}. The same resource can be relevant to one task for its evaluation design
and to another for its implementation. The system must expose useful context
across materials while preserving the distinctions that determine whether a
particular paper, dataset, or tool fits the current question. A useful account
of a research area must still allow the agent to locate and inspect individual
resources.

Third, \emph{reuse must preserve the basis for further inspection}. Condensed
accounts can omit qualifications, and external materials can change after they
are examined. The library therefore needs to retain source references,
applicability conditions, and the scope of any checks. A paper's claim that code
is released, observation of a repository, and successful execution provide
different kinds of evidence. An agent preparing to use a resource must be able
to tell which evidence is available and which questions remain unresolved.

We propose \emph{Papers for Agents (P4A)} as a system for constructing and
accessing reusable research knowledge from published papers and their associated
resources. Its goal is to help agents discover relevant work, assess its fit to
a research need, select supporting resources, and prepare to use them. P4A is
designed around a persistent library that brings together resource descriptions,
research context, documented uses, and supporting sources. Agents perform the
material interpretation needed to construct this knowledge, while downstream
research agents access it and return to original materials when further
inspection is required. The system focuses on information available in published
materials; recovering unreported research history is outside this scope.

Our research agenda has three parts. The first is a resource representation
that captures the knowledge needed for discovery, assessment, and use while
preserving its evidential basis. The second is a construction and access
framework that makes this knowledge reusable across research tasks. The third
is a system evaluation connecting the quality of the constructed library to
resource discovery, justified selection, and preparation for use. We plan to
compare against agents with equivalent access to source materials and existing
resource services, measure construction and downstream costs, and examine which
design choices account for any improvements. This evaluation will test whether
preparing reusable research knowledge improves research decisions and reduces
repeated reading and interpretation enough to justify its construction cost.
