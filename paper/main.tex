\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Research agents need access to a range of scholarly resources, including papers,
datasets, benchmarks, and code repositories. Consider an agent seeking an
evaluation setup for long-context instruction following. It must understand what
a benchmark measures, identify suitable data, and locate the corresponding
evaluation tools. These resources describe different aspects of the same research
activity. Supporting such tasks therefore requires helping agents discover
relevant resources and understand what each can contribute to their work. This
perspective complements efforts to reconsider how research artifacts are
delivered to agents~\cite{2026-ARA}; here, we focus on organizing resources from
already published research.

Resource discovery in this setting requires connecting individual descriptions
with the broader context in which resources are useful. A paper may explain an
evaluation's motivation, a dataset page may describe its contents, and a
repository may document how to run the evaluation. Their descriptions can differ
substantially in vocabulary and emphasis. A query may retrieve the benchmark
paper while leaving the agent to locate the data and tools and determine their
roles. This raises a question: can a representation of a collection provide
useful retrieval context beyond the descriptions of its individual resources?

We explore \emph{resource communities} as units of organization, with
agent-generated summaries serving as retrieval entry points. A community groups
resources around a shared research topic or use case. Its summary synthesizes
member materials to describe the collection's scope, resources, and intended
uses, with references to the supporting sources. In the evaluation example, a
community summary could explain the evaluation problem and identify which
members provide task definitions, data, and evaluation tools. Retrieval can then
use this collective context to locate relevant resources, while individual
records retain the information needed for further inspection and use.

Making this approach useful presents three challenges. First, \emph{community
formation must reflect what makes resources useful together}. Textual similarity
alone may not capture this relationship: a method paper and its implementation
can have very different descriptions, while resources with similar terminology
may address different tasks or experimental settings. A central design question
is whether, and how, complementarity in a research task should inform community
formation. Community boundaries also matter. Broad communities may obscure
specific needs, whereas narrow communities may offer little context beyond an
individual resource.

Second, \emph{summaries must preserve distinctions among community members}.
A summary that emphasizes only the dominant theme may omit a resource that is
important for a particular query. It may also incorrectly attribute one member's
capabilities to the entire collection. For example, the presence of evaluation
code does not establish that every method in a community has an executable
implementation. Specific statements must remain attributable to the resources
and materials that support them, with uncertainty preserved where evidence is
insufficient. Summary quality thus depends both on factual support and on
retaining the information needed to distinguish and select individual resources.

Third, \emph{community retrieval must lead to relevant individual resources}.
Matching a query to a topical summary does not establish that the retrieved
collection contains the data or tools the task requires. Retrieval must use
community context to help identify suitable members without hiding resources
whose distinctive features are absent from the summary. This requires examining
how summary-based retrieval should interact with direct resource retrieval.
Success must ultimately be measured by the resources discovered and their fit
to the task, rather than by the fluency or topical relevance of the summary.

We investigate these questions through the design of \emph{P4A}, a system for
organizing and retrieving research resources for agents. Given resource records
and source materials for papers, datasets, benchmarks, and repositories, P4A
aims to connect community formation, grounded summarization, and resource
retrieval. Agents interpret materials to organize resources and construct
summaries; downstream research agents inspect and use the retrieved resources.
Our central question is how to form useful resource communities and summarize
them faithfully while preserving member distinctions, so that knowledge about a
collection improves the discovery of its constituent resources.

Our planned evaluation examines community and summary quality, resource
retrieval effectiveness, and the costs of construction and use. Comparisons with
direct resource retrieval, simple clustering followed by summarization, and
related community-based retrieval methods will control access to source
materials and model budgets. Component analyses will distinguish the effects of
resource organization from those of summarization. Together, these evaluations
are intended to establish whether community representations help agents find
resources that better satisfy their needs, which design choices account for any
gains, and whether those gains justify the cost of constructing the collection
knowledge.
